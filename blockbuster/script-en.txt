Dallas, the year 2000. Two young founders walk into Blockbuster's headquarters and offer to sell their tiny company for fifty million dollars. Across the table, Blockbuster's CEO is trying very hard not to laugh.

That tiny company was Netflix. Today, it's worth roughly three hundred billion dollars. And Blockbuster? Its entire empire now fits inside one single store, in a small town in Oregon.

At its peak, Blockbuster made around eight hundred million dollars a year from late fees alone. That's more than two million dollars a day, from people who forgot to return a movie.

But here's what almost nobody tells you. Blockbuster didn't die because it ignored the internet. It saw it coming. It fought back. And for a moment, it was actually winning.

Until a billionaire investor walked into the boardroom.

Dallas, Texas. October 19, 1985.

A man named David Cook opens a video rental store unlike anything people have seen. Bright lights. Wide aisles. Around eight thousand tapes on the shelves, and a computer system to check them out. Video stores back then were usually small, dark and a little sketchy. Blockbuster felt like a supermarket for movies.

Then a businessman named Wayne Huizenga takes over and turns it into a machine. New stores everywhere. Make it a Blockbuster night. Friday evening becomes a ritual. You walk the aisles, you fight your siblings over the box, and the one movie you actually wanted is always, somehow, rented out.

"Do you have the new Jurassic Park?"
"All forty copies are out."
"Forty?"
"Forty. Want a documentary about bees?"

In 1994, the media giant Viacom buys Blockbuster for eight point four billion dollars. By 2004, there are nine thousand and ninety-four Blockbuster stores around the world, and more than eighty-four thousand employees.

And here's the dirty secret of the business model: late fees. Return your tape a day late, and you pay. Blockbuster reportedly made about eight hundred million dollars a year this way. Roughly sixteen percent of its revenue. Translation: one dollar out of every six came from punishing its own customers.

"That's four dollars for the rental."
"Great."
"And eight dollars of late fees."
"For a movie I didn't even like?"
"Especially for that one."

Then came the red envelope.

In 1997, two entrepreneurs in California, Reed Hastings and Marc Randolph, start a company that rents DVDs by mail. No store. No driving. You pick a movie on a website, and it shows up in your mailbox. They call it Netflix.

There's a famous legend that Hastings started Netflix out of rage after paying a forty-dollar late fee on Apollo 13. Great story. But his co-founder later called it a convenient fiction. Basically, branding. Very Hollywood.

Still, in 2000, Netflix is losing money and needs help. So Hastings and Randolph fly to Dallas with an offer. Buy us for fifty million dollars. You run the stores. We'll run the online business for you.

According to Randolph, Blockbuster's CEO, John Antioco, was struggling not to laugh. The meeting went downhill fast. No deal.

But here's the twist almost everyone forgets. Blockbuster wasn't ignoring the internet at all. That same year, in July 2000, it signed a twenty-year deal to stream movies over the internet. Its partner? A hot, innovative energy company from Houston called Enron.

Yes. That Enron. Cue the ominous music.

They tested it with about a thousand homes in four cities. And by March 2001, eight months later, the twenty-year deal was already over. A few months after that, Enron collapsed in one of the biggest frauds in American history. Blockbuster's first streaming partner: a company built on fake numbers. You can't make this up.

And then, Antioco does something bold.

In 2004, Blockbuster launches its own online rental service, Blockbuster Online. And on January 1, 2005, it kills late fees. Gone. Just like that. A huge revenue stream, thrown away on purpose, to win back customers.

Then, in late 2006, comes the masterstroke: Total Access. Rent DVDs online, like Netflix. But when you're done, drop them at any Blockbuster store and walk out with a new movie, instantly. Netflix had mailboxes. Blockbuster had mailboxes and nine thousand stores.

It works. By the end of 2006, Blockbuster Online has about two million subscribers. According to the book Netflixed, new sign-ups suddenly swing toward Blockbuster. By the end of January 2007, Netflix's stock has dropped almost twelve percent. For the first time, the little red envelope is scared.

Picture it. The dinosaur is learning to fly.

But then came the billionaire.

Enter Carl Icahn, one of Wall Street's most famous activist investors. Translation: someone who buys a big chunk of a company's shares and then pressures management to do things his way. By 2005, he owns nearly ten percent of Blockbuster and wins three seats on the board.

And Icahn hates the plan. The online service is expensive. Killing late fees cost a fortune. Antioco's bonus? Way too big, according to Icahn.

"We're finally beating Netflix."
"Wonderful. How much is it costing us?"
"A lot."
"Then stop beating them so expensively."

The fight gets ugly. And in 2007, Antioco is out.

His replacement is Jim Keyes, the former boss of 7-Eleven. His priority: pay down debt and bring focus back to the stores. Marketing for the online service gets cut. Growth stalls. Antioco later said he believed Blockbuster Online could have reached ten million subscribers if it had kept going.

Meanwhile, in January 2007, Netflix quietly launches something new: streaming. Watch instantly. No envelope. No store. No late fees. Ever.

You can probably guess the rest.

September 23, 2010. Blockbuster files for bankruptcy, with about one point five billion dollars of debt. In 2011, the satellite TV company Dish buys what's left, including about seventeen hundred stores, in a deal valued at around three hundred and twenty million dollars. The stores close, one after another.

Until only one is left.

Bend, Oregon. By 2019, this is the last Blockbuster store on the planet. And instead of disappearing, it becomes a tourist attraction. People fly in to take selfies. Buy t-shirts. Rent a DVD for old times' sake. In 2020, the store was even listed on Airbnb, for four dollars a night. It got its own documentary. And in 2026, it's still open.

And Netflix? More than three hundred million paying members. Worth roughly three hundred billion dollars. The company Blockbuster could have bought for fifty million.

So what's the lesson? Blockbuster didn't fail because it couldn't see the future. It saw it, built it, and was winning. And then it chose short-term numbers over the long game. Sometimes the most expensive decision isn't the one you refuse. It's the one you abandon halfway.

Fifty million dollars. One laugh. And the last laugh went to the red envelope.
