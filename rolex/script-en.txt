July 6, 1960. A seventy-nine-year-old man dies in Geneva. He leaves behind the most famous watch brand on Earth. And he leaves it to nobody.

He started out as an orphan from a small town in Bavaria. He ended up building the watch that movie stars, deep-sea explorers and half of all rap lyrics are obsessed with.

Today, analysts estimate Rolex sells about ten billion Swiss francs of watches a year. Around one point two million watches. That's one new Rolex roughly every twenty-seven seconds.

And yet, nobody owns Rolex. No shareholders. No stock price. No billionaire heir with a yacht named after a watch. So where does all that money go?

The answer starts with a grieving widower, and ends with a charity that hands out hundreds of millions a year without making a sound.

Kulmbach, Bavaria. 1881.

Hans Wilsdorf is born into a family of hardware merchants. At twelve, he loses his mother. A few months later, his father. His uncles sell the family business and lock the money away until the kids grow up. Not exactly a Disney intro. Although, to be fair, Disney intros are mostly orphans.

He moves to London. And in 1905, at twenty-four, he starts a watch company with his brother-in-law, Alfred Davis. Wilsdorf and Davis. Catchy. We'll fix that later.

His big bet? The wristwatch. Here's the thing: back then, real men carried pocket watches. Wristwatches were small, inaccurate, and seen as jewelry for women.

"A watch... on your wrist?"
"Yes."
"Like a bracelet?"
"Like the future."
"Sir, I would sooner wear a skirt."

Wilsdorf doesn't care. He's convinced the wristwatch will take over. He just needs two things. A name. And proof.

The name first. He says he combined letters every possible way, got about a hundred names, and none of them worked. Then, one morning, riding on the top deck of a horse-drawn bus through the City of London, in his own words, a genie whispered in his ear: Rolex. Short. Easy to say in any language. Looks good on a dial. In 1908, the name is registered. Branding department: one guy, one bus, one genie.

Now the proof. In 1910, a Rolex becomes the first wristwatch in the world to earn the Swiss certificate of chronometric precision. Translation: an official accuracy test, like a driving exam, but for watches. Then, in 1914, the Kew Observatory in England gives a tiny Rolex a Class A certificate. That's a test usually reserved for big marine chronometers on ships. Forty-five days. Five positions. Three temperatures. And the little wristwatch passes. The bracelet just beat the boat clocks.

But then comes World War One. Britain slaps heavy taxes on luxury imports and duties on the gold and silver used for watch cases. So in 1919, Wilsdorf packs up and moves the company to Geneva. Tax planning, nineteen-nineteen style.

And this is where it gets interesting.

Watches back then had a weakness: water and dust got inside and killed them. In 1926, Rolex patents a case with a screwed-down bezel, back and crown, the little knob you wind it with. Sealed shut, like an oyster. So he calls it the Oyster. Naming is clearly his superpower now.

But how do you prove a watch is waterproof in 1927? You find someone crazy enough to swim the English Channel.

Enter Mercedes Gleitze. A typist from London. On October 7, 1927, she swims the Channel. But then another woman claims a faster swim, and doubts spread. So two weeks later, Gleitze gets back in the water to prove herself. This time, a gold Rolex Oyster hangs on a ribbon around her neck. More than ten hours in cold water. And the watch? Still ticking. Totally dry inside.

Wilsdorf doesn't waste it. On November 24, 1927, Rolex buys the front page of the Daily Mail. The wonder watch that defies the elements. Influencer marketing, almost a century before Instagram.

In 1931, the Perpetual rotor. Translation: the watch winds itself every time your wrist moves. In 1945, the Datejust, with a date that changes on its own.

But then came the darkest chapter.

World War Two. British officers captured by Germany have their watches taken away. So Wilsdorf makes them an offer. Write to Rolex through the Red Cross, order a watch, and pay after the war. He reportedly told them: "You must not even think of settlement during the war." Officers in the camps ordered as many as three thousand watches.

"One Oyster, please. I'll pay you after the war."
"Of course, sir."
"And if we lose the war?"
"Then, sir, the invoice will be the least of our problems."

Generous. Patriotic. And honestly, the greatest customer loyalty program ever invented.

Then, in 1944, his wife Florence dies. They have no children. No heirs. So Wilsdorf does something almost no founder does. He creates a foundation, the Hans Wilsdorf Foundation. Translation: a kind of charity that can own things, but has no owners itself. And he arranges for it to own Rolex. When he dies, his entire stake goes to it.

Fast forward to January 23, 1960. The deep-sea submersible Trieste dives to the bottom of the Mariana Trench. Ten thousand nine hundred and sixteen meters. The deepest point on the planet. Strapped to the outside: a Rolex. When it comes back up, the watch works perfectly. The explorer Jacques Piccard sends a telegram to Geneva: your watch works as well at eleven thousand meters as it does on the surface.

Less than six months later, on July 6, 1960, Hans Wilsdorf dies. The orphan from Bavaria leaves his empire to a foundation. And Rolex officially belongs to nobody.

So, what happens to a company with no owner?

It gets bigger. Much bigger.

October 26, 2017. New York. Paul Newman's own Rolex Daytona, a gift from his wife, goes up for auction. It sells for seventeen point eight million dollars. At the time, the most expensive wristwatch ever sold at auction. For a watch that tells you the time. Just like your phone.

In 2023, Rolex buys Bucherer, a famous watch retailer it had worked with since 1924. And according to estimates from Morgan Stanley and LuxeConsult, in 2024 Rolex sold about ten point five billion Swiss francs of watches. Roughly a third of the entire Swiss watch market. Rolex itself publishes no accounts. Peak mystery.

"How much for Rolex?"
"It's not for sale."
"Everything is for sale."
"Not this. Nobody owns it."
"Then who do I talk to?"
"Nobody."

Here's the trick. No shareholders means nobody calling every three months asking for more growth. Translation: Rolex can think in decades, not quarters. It can make fewer watches than people want. And waiting lists are the best advertising money can't buy.

And the foundation? According to its own figures, it pays about seventy-five million francs in taxes a year, and in one recent year it gave out more than five hundred million francs in aid. Housing, education, social causes, mostly in Geneva. Its motto, translated from French: good does not make noise, and noise does no good. Very Swiss.

So what's the lesson? Sometimes the most powerful thing a founder can do is make sure nobody can ever cash out.

An orphan who had no heirs built a company that can never be inherited, never be sold, and never stops ticking.
