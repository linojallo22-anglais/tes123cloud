Every year, McDonald's collects ten billion dollars in rent. Not from selling burgers. From being a landlord.

And the man who built that machine was a fifty-two-year-old milkshake mixer salesman with diabetes, arthritis and no gallbladder.

Ten billion dollars a year. That's about nineteen thousand dollars every single minute. In rent.

So here's the question. If McDonald's is really a landlord, then what exactly is it selling you?

And the two brothers who invented the whole thing? They ended up with one restaurant, no name, and a promise that, according to their family, was never kept.

San Bernardino, California. 1948.

Brothers Dick and Mac McDonald run a busy drive-in. Carhops, a big menu. Then they do something insane. They shut it down. They rebuild the kitchen. Goodbye, carhops. The menu shrinks to nine items, and a hamburger costs fifteen cents.

"Excuse me, where's the waitress?"
"Gone. Walk up to the window."
"And the menu?"
"Hamburger. Cheeseburger. You're welcome."

They call it the Speedee Service System. Translation: an assembly line, like Henry Ford's car factory, but for burgers. Every worker does one job. Grill. Fries. Shakes. Again and again. Picture a Formula One pit stop where everybody smells like onions.

It works. The lines go out the door. And two thousand miles away, a salesman notices something strange in his order book.

Enter Ray Kroc.

Born in 1902, near Chicago. At fifteen, he lies about his age to drive a Red Cross ambulance in World War One. During training, he meets another young driver named Walt Disney. Totally normal people to hang out with.

After the war, Kroc tries everything. Piano player. Real estate. Then years selling paper cups for the Lily-Tulip company. Finally, he goes all in on one product: the Multimixer, a machine that makes five milkshakes at once.

Then, in 1954, he hears about a little restaurant in California using eight of his machines. Eight. That's forty milkshakes at the same time. For one burger stand. Even a stadium doesn't need that much.

So Kroc goes to see it for himself. And here's where it gets interesting. He doesn't see a restaurant. He sees a photocopier. If it works here, it works everywhere.

The brothers already license a few restaurants, but Kroc offers to take franchising nationwide. Translation: local owners pay to use the name and the system. The deal: each new franchise pays nine hundred and fifty dollars, and Kroc collects one point nine percent of every restaurant's sales. Half a percent goes to the brothers.

April 15, 1955. Kroc opens his own McDonald's in Des Plaines, Illinois. He's fifty-two. In his memoir, he lists his health like a grocery list: diabetes, arthritis, no gallbladder, most of his thyroid gone. And then he says the best is still ahead of him. Peak salesman energy.

But there's a problem.

"We sold another franchise!"
"Great. Minus the trainers, the inspectors, the travel..."
"So how much did we make?"
"Do you want the honest number, or the motivating one?"

Kroc pays for training and inspectors. His one point four percent barely covers it. Worse, the contract says he can't change the system unless both brothers approve it in writing. Want to tweak something? Send a letter. Wait. Hope. Kroc is building an empire on a salary of crumbs. Spoiler: those crumbs are about to turn into bricks.

Enter Harry Sonneborn.

A finance man from the ice cream chain Tastee-Freez. He asks one simple question. What if the money isn't in the burgers?

In the mid-fifties, they set up a separate company called Franchise Realty Corporation. Here's the plan. McDonald's leases or buys the land, puts up the restaurant, then rents the whole package to the franchisee. With a markup. Reportedly as high as forty percent.

And the rent? A fixed minimum, or a percentage of sales, whichever is higher. Translation: if the restaurant does badly, McDonald's still gets paid. If it does great, McDonald's gets paid more. Heads I win, tails you pay rent.

"Congratulations, here's your restaurant!"
"Amazing. And the land?"
"Oh, the land is ours. Rent is due on the first."
"Welcome to the family."

Sonneborn later put it bluntly. "We are not technically in the food business. We are in the real estate business."

And it works. In 1958, there are thirty-four McDonald's. In 1959 alone, Kroc opens sixty-seven more. Sonneborn becomes the company's first president. There's just one thing Kroc still doesn't own. The name.

Fast forward to 1961.

Kroc wants the brothers out. He asks for their price. The answer: two point seven million dollars. Cash. Enough for one million each, after taxes.

"They want two point seven million."
"For a name?"
"For a name, and a menu with nine items."

Kroc reportedly went ballistic. But Sonneborn finds the money, including a one-and-a-half-million-dollar loan from three insurance companies.

Then, at the closing table, a twist. The brothers are keeping their original restaurant. They're giving it to their employees. Kroc is furious. According to the McDonald family, there was also a handshake promise: a share of future sales, forever. It was never written down. And it was never paid.

The brothers' restaurant can't be called McDonald's anymore, so it becomes The Big M. And Kroc? He opens a brand-new McDonald's one block away. Cue the ominous music. Six years later, the Big M closes. This is the Game of Thrones chapter of the story. Minus the dragons.

That same year, 1961, McDonald's opens Hamburger University, in the basement of a restaurant in Elk Grove Village, Illinois. The first class: fifteen graduates. Their degree? Hamburgerology. Yes, really.

Then came the money.

April 21, 1965. McDonald's goes public. Translation: anyone can now buy a piece of it. Twenty-two dollars and fifty cents a share. By the end of day one, it's around thirty. Kroc buys a ranch. As one does.

In 1967, a franchisee near Pittsburgh named Jim Delligatti wants a bigger burger for adults. Two patties, three buns, special sauce. The Big Mac. The company's most famous product came from a franchisee, not headquarters.

And Sonneborn? That same year, he's convinced a recession is coming. Kroc wants to keep building. They clash. On June 8, 1967, Sonneborn resigns, and sells his stock for several million dollars. That stock became worth far more. The real estate genius, beaten by his own timing.

Ray Kroc dies on January 14, 1984. By then: seven thousand five hundred restaurants, his own baseball team, the San Diego Padres, and the word founder next to his name in a lot of history books.

And today?

More than forty-five thousand restaurants. About ninety-five percent run by franchisees. In 2024, McDonald's collected ten billion dollars in rent, and five point six billion in royalties, its cut of sales. Rent wins by almost two to one. On its books, the company's land and buildings add up to more than forty-one billion dollars. And that's at the price it originally paid.

And the very first McDonald's site in San Bernardino? The one piece of land Kroc never got? In 1998, after the owner couldn't pay, it sold at a foreclosure auction for one hundred and thirty-five thousand dollars. The buyer was the founder of a chicken restaurant chain. He turned it into an unofficial McDonald's museum.

So what's the lesson? Sometimes the product is just the excuse. The real business is whatever sits underneath it.

Nineteen thousand dollars a minute. In rent. The burger was never the point.
