December 2, 2001. The seventh-largest company in America files for bankruptcy. The biggest bankruptcy the country has ever seen.

Six years in a row, Fortune magazine had named it America's most innovative company. Its official values were respect, integrity, communication and excellence. Spoiler: it was missing a few.

The year before, Enron reported almost one hundred and one billion dollars in revenue. That's about two hundred and seventy-seven million dollars a day. On paper.

So how does a giant that big disappear in a few weeks?

The answer involves Star Wars, dinosaurs, a paper shredder, and four boring words that nearly broke Wall Street: mark-to-market accounting.

Houston, Texas. 1985.

Two gas pipeline companies merge. The new boss is Ken Lay, an economist with big political friends and an even bigger smile. The new company gets a name: Enron. And for a few years, it does something incredibly boring. It moves natural gas through pipes. Nobody gets rich telling that story at parties.

Then came Jeff Skilling.

A brilliant consultant from McKinsey, Skilling has an idea: what if Enron stopped just moving gas, and started trading it, like stocks on Wall Street? A gas bank. Buy, sell, bet on prices.

But he has one condition before joining. Enron must use something called mark-to-market accounting. Translation: when you sign a twenty-year deal, you count all the profit you hope to make over twenty years, on day one. Not when the money arrives. Today.

"So this contract makes money over twenty years?"
"Yes."
"And we book all of it today?"
"Today."
"And if it never makes money?"
"Then we'll need a bigger contract."

On January 30, 1992, the regulator, the SEC, says it won't object. Skilling is so happy, he celebrates with champagne. Champagne. For an accounting rule.

And here's the thing. That rule turns Enron into a machine that has to invent bigger and bigger deals every quarter, just to keep the numbers growing. Like running on a treadmill that speeds up every three months.

But for a while, it looks like magic.

Enron trades gas, electricity, paper, even internet bandwidth. In 1999, it pays more than one hundred million dollars, over thirty years, to put its name on the Houston Astros' new stadium: Enron Field. In August 2000, its stock hits about ninety dollars a share. And in July 2000, remember, it even signs a twenty-year deal with Blockbuster to stream movies. That one lasted eight months. Foreshadowing.

But there's a problem. Many of these deals are losing money. And losses look terrible on paper.

Enter Andrew Fastow, Enron's chief financial officer. His job: make the losses disappear. His tool: special purpose entities. Translation: separate companies, created on the side, where Enron can quietly park its bad assets and its debts, so they don't show up on its own books. Out of sight, out of mind.

And the names? This is where it gets weird. One partnership was called JEDI. Another one, created to help keep JEDI off the books, was called Chewco. As in Chewbacca. Later came a group of entities called the Raptors. As in the dinosaurs. Somewhere in Houston, someone was having way too much fun.

"Where did we put the losses?"
"Somewhere safe."
"Where?"
"Chewco."
"Like... Chewbacca?"
"Don't ask questions you don't want answered."

In June 1999, Enron's own board even allowed Fastow to run one of these partnerships himself, called LJM. Translation: the man in charge of Enron's money was also on the other side of the deals. Totally normal. Definitely not a conflict of interest.

Then came California.

In 2000 and 2001, the state goes through an energy crisis. Prices explode. Rolling blackouts hit millions of people. Later, recordings revealed Enron traders joking about the money they were making from California, talking about ripping off "Grandma Millie." Cue the ominous music.

And then, the cracks start to show.

In February 2001, Skilling becomes CEO. In April, on a call with Wall Street, an investor asks a simple question: why is Enron the only financial company that can't show a balance sheet with its earnings? Skilling answers with an insult. A word we can't say on this channel. Fun fact: Enron's advertising slogan at the time was "Ask why." Just, maybe, not out loud.

Four months later, on August 14, he suddenly resigns. The very next day, an Enron vice president named Sherron Watkins sends Ken Lay an anonymous memo. Her warning: the company could "implode in a wave of accounting scandals."

Lay takes the CEO job back. And on September 26, 2001, in an online chat with employees, he says Enron's stock is "an incredible bargain." Buy, buy, buy.

Three weeks later, on October 16, Enron announces a six hundred and eighteen million dollar loss, and a one point two billion dollar hit to shareholders' equity, partly tied to Fastow's partnerships. In November, it admits its profits had been overstated going back to 1997. A smaller rival, Dynegy, agrees to buy Enron at a bargain price. Then it reads the books, and walks away on November 28. That day, the stock falls below one dollar.

From ninety dollars to less than one. In about fifteen months.

December 2, 2001. Enron files for bankruptcy.

And the people who pay the price are not the executives. At the end of 2000, employees had about two point one billion dollars in their retirement plan. Sixty-two percent of it was in Enron stock. Thousands lose their jobs and a huge part of their savings at the same time.

But the story isn't over.

Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, one of the biggest accounting firms in the world, is caught shredding Enron documents. In June 2002, it's convicted. Three years later, the Supreme Court overturns the conviction. Too late. The firm has already collapsed, and around twenty-eight thousand people in the US are out of work.

Washington reacts too. In 2002, Congress passes the Sarbanes-Oxley Act. Translation: new rules forcing company bosses to personally sign off on their numbers. If the numbers lie, it's on them.

Andrew Fastow pleads guilty and gets six years in prison. Jeff Skilling and Ken Lay are convicted in May 2006. Skilling is sentenced to twenty-four years, serves about twelve, and is released in 2019. Ken Lay never goes to prison. He dies of a heart attack on July 5, 2006, before his sentencing, and his conviction is erased.

And Enron Field? The Astros buy the name back for about two point one million dollars, and rename the stadium. Even the ballpark wanted a divorce.

But here's the twist. In 2020, a comedy group reportedly bought the Enron brand for two hundred and seventy-five dollars. And in December 2024, they relaunched it, as a parody. Their slogan: "We're back. Can we talk?"

So what's the lesson? When a company's numbers are too complicated to explain, sometimes that's not genius. It's the point.

Respect. Integrity. Communication. Excellence. Four words on a wall. Zero on the books.
