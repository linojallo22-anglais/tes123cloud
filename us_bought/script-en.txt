In 1803, the United States doubled in size. Overnight. It paid about three cents an acre.
In 1867, it bought a land more than twice the size of Texas. People called it a folly.
And in 1946, it secretly tried to buy the biggest island on Earth. With gold.
America didn't only grow by fighting. It grew by shopping.
This is every time America bought land. And at the end, you'll see which deal was the best, and which one was the most expensive.

Part one. The deal of the century.
It's 1803. The United States is a young country squeezed against the Atlantic. Everything west of the Mississippi River belongs to France. And France belongs to one man: Napoleon.
America only wants one city: New Orleans. Every farmer in the West ships his crops down the Mississippi, and New Orleans is the door to the sea.
So President Thomas Jefferson sends his diplomats to Paris, with permission to spend up to ten million dollars for New Orleans and a piece of Florida.
But Napoleon has a problem. His army has just lost a war in Haiti, and a new war with Britain is coming. He needs money. Fast.
So the French make an offer nobody expected. Napoleon's minister asks the Americans: what would you give for the whole of Louisiana? Why buy a city, when you can buy everything?
Eight hundred and twenty-eight thousand square miles. For fifteen million dollars. About three cents an acre.
With one signature, the United States doubled in size. This land would later become all or part of fifteen states, from Louisiana to Montana. One of the American diplomats, Robert Livingston, said it was the noblest work of their whole lives.
And here's the twist. America didn't have the cash. So it borrowed from two banks: Baring Brothers in London, and Hope and Company in Amsterdam. Translation: British bankers helped pay Napoleon, just as he went back to war with Britain.

Part two. The South, the war, and a railroad.
Next on the list: Florida. In 1819, Spain is too weak to hold it, and hands it over in the Adams-Onís Treaty. America doesn't even write Spain a check. Instead, it agrees to pay up to five million dollars of claims that American citizens had against Spain.
Then comes the biggest, and the most controversial. In 1846, the United States goes to war with Mexico. Two years later, the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo hands over about five hundred thousand square miles: California, Nevada, Utah, and large parts of Arizona, New Mexico and Colorado. In return, the United States pays Mexico fifteen million dollars. A purchase, yes. But signed after a war. Counting Texas, Mexico lost about half of its territory.
And the timing is unbelievable. On January twenty-fourth, 1848, gold is found at Sutter's Mill, in California. Nine days later, the treaty is signed. Mexico had just given away the land of the Gold Rush, without knowing it.
But the border still wasn't finished. Look at this strip at the bottom of Arizona and New Mexico. In 1853, the United States buys it for ten million dollars. Why? A railroad. Engineers said the best southern route to California ran through this flat desert. It's called the Gadsden Purchase. The city of Tucson came with it. And it's why the border looks like this today.

Part three. Seward's Icebox.
1867. Russia owns a giant piece of North America: Alaska. But it's far from the Russian capital, hard to defend, and Russia fears it could lose it to Britain anyway. So it offers to sell.
The American Secretary of State, William Seward, negotiates through the night, and the deal is signed at four in the morning. The price: seven point two million dollars, for five hundred and eighty-six thousand square miles. About two cents an acre.
Alaska is more than twice the size of Texas. Put it on top of the lower forty-eight, and with its islands, it stretches almost from coast to coast.
But back then, critics laughed. Newspapers called it Seward's Icebox. Some even called it Walrussia.
Then came gold, in the 1890s. And in 1968, the biggest oil field in North America, at Prudhoe Bay. The icebox was a treasure chest.
And one more thing. From Little Diomede Island, in Alaska, you can see Russia. Big Diomede is only about two and a half miles away.

Part four. The most expensive acre.
Not every deal was cheap. In 1917, during the First World War, the United States buys three small Caribbean islands from Denmark: Saint Thomas, Saint John and Saint Croix. Today, they're the U.S. Virgin Islands.
The price: twenty-five million dollars, in gold. For about one hundred and thirty-four square miles. That's almost three hundred dollars an acre. Compared to Louisiana, about ten thousand times more expensive.
Why pay so much? Fear. Washington worried that Germany could take the islands and use them as a submarine base, close to the Panama Canal.
And there's one purchase America later let go. In 1898, after a war with Spain, the United States pays Spain twenty million dollars for the Philippines: more than seven thousand islands. In 1946, the Philippines became independent.
Same story in Panama. In 1904, the United States paid ten million dollars to control a strip of land ten miles wide, to build the Panama Canal. In 1999, it handed it back.

Part five. The one that got away.
And then, there's Greenland. In 1946, President Harry Truman's government secretly offered Denmark one hundred million dollars in gold for the biggest island on Earth. Denmark said no.
Greenland is huge: about eight hundred and thirty-six thousand square miles. Bigger than the entire Louisiana Purchase. But only about fifty-six thousand people live there.
In 2019, and again in 2025, the idea came back. And the answer from Denmark and Greenland was the same: Greenland is not for sale.
Why does America want it so much? Look at the map. Greenland sits between North America and Europe, on the shortest route over the Arctic. Translation: whoever holds Greenland watches the top of the world.

So, which deal was the best?
At about two cents an acre, Alaska was the cheapest. Louisiana doubled the country for three cents. And the Virgin Islands cost almost three hundred dollars an acre.
Add it all up, and more than half of today's United States came from deals that ended with a payment.
A city that became half a continent, a desert bought for a railroad, an icebox full of gold, and an island that said no.
